CHAPTER 1
THE DECISION TO GO NUCLEAR
In December 2020, Rwanda made a decisive commitment. Presidential Order 127/01 formally established the Rwanda Atomic Energy Board, the institutional engine of a national nuclear programme. This was not a study or an expression of interest. It was a structural act that embedded nuclear energy into Rwanda's sovereign development architecture. Nuclear power was written into the national energy policy, the national development strategy, and the National Land-Use Master Plan. President Kagame's framing was direct: nuclear is the platform for industrial growth, not just a power source.

EDUCATION / INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERS
LEARNING HUB
For students, educators, policymakers, and any first-time visitor. Plain-language explainers on nuclear energy, the IAEA process, financing structures, and why Rwanda's approach is distinctive.
01
Fission: The Core Process
Nuclear energy is produced by splitting uranium atoms in a controlled process called fission. When a uranium-235 atom absorbs a neutron, it splits into smaller atoms and releases a large amount of energy as heat. No combustion occurs, so there are no carbon dioxide emissions during operation.
02
Heat to Electricity
The heat from fission is used to produce steam. Steam drives a turbine connected to a generator, which produces electricity. This is the same process used in coal and gas plants, but the fuel source is nuclear rather than fossil fuels.
03
The Chain Reaction
Each fission event releases additional neutrons, which can split more uranium atoms. This self-sustaining chain reaction is carefully controlled by inserting or withdrawing control rods. A nuclear plant cannot explode like a nuclear bomb because the fuel is not enriched to the required level.
04
Waste Volume
The volume of high-level nuclear waste produced is remarkably small. All the spent fuel ever produced by US commercial nuclear reactors would fit inside a single Walmart. The waste is solid, dense, and can be safely stored in sealed containers for very long periods.
01
What is an SMR
A Small Modular Reactor generates between 10 and 300 MW of electricity, compared to 1,000 MW or more for a conventional large reactor. SMRs are built in factories and transported to site, reducing construction time and cost. Rwanda's target is units of 110 to 150 MW per module.
02
Why SMRs Suit Africa
Africa's electricity grids are smaller than those in Europe or North America. A 1,000 MW reactor would overwhelm most African grids. SMRs can be scaled incrementally, adding capacity as demand grows. They require less water and have smaller site footprints, making them suitable for landlocked countries like Rwanda.
03
Passive Safety
Most SMR designs feature passive safety systems. In an emergency, cooling happens automatically through physics: gravity, natural circulation, and heat transfer, without needing electricity or human action. The reactor shuts down safely on its own, unlike older reactor designs.
04
Financing Advantage
A 77 MW SMR module might cost USD 200 to 300 million, compared to USD 10 billion or more for a large conventional reactor. This lower upfront cost makes financing more accessible. Countries can also stage investment, adding modules as revenue from early units begins to flow.
01
The Milestones Approach
The IAEA provides a structured three-phase framework for countries developing nuclear programmes. Phase 1 is Consider: readiness to commit. Phase 2 is Prepare: ready to invite bids. Phase 3 is Construct: ready to commission. Rwanda has completed Phase 1.
02
The INIR Mission
An Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review is a formal assessment conducted by international experts appointed by the IAEA. They evaluate the country across 19 specific infrastructure areas including safety, security, safeguards, legal framework, waste management, and public information. Rwanda's INIR took place 2 to 9 March 2026.
03
The 19 Infrastructure Areas
These include: national position, nuclear safety, management, funding and financing, legal framework, safeguards, regulatory framework, radiation protection, electrical grid, human resource development, stakeholder involvement, site and supporting facilities, environmental protection, emergency planning, nuclear security, nuclear fuel cycle, radioactive waste management, industrial involvement, and procurement.
04
What Good Practices Mean
When the IAEA identifies a good practice, it means Rwanda's approach in that area is considered a model that other countries should study and replicate. The March 2026 mission identified Rwanda's government coordination, stakeholder engagement, and emergency preparedness as good practices. This is a significant institutional endorsement.
01
The Financing Challenge
Nuclear plants have high upfront capital costs, typically 70 to 85 percent fixed before a single kilowatt-hour is generated. This front-loaded cost profile is the primary barrier for developing countries. SMRs reduce this barrier significantly, with a 100 MW unit estimated at USD 200 to 300 million vs USD 5 to 10 billion for a large reactor.
02
Blended Finance
Most nuclear projects in emerging markets use blended finance: a combination of government equity, development finance institution (DFI) loans, export credit agency (ECA) guarantees from the vendor country, and commercial debt. Each layer de-risks the investment for the next. Rwanda's model will likely follow this structure.
03
Power Purchase Agreements
A PPA is a long-term contract between the plant owner and an electricity buyer, typically 20 to 30 years. PPAs provide revenue certainty, which enables lenders to commit financing. Industrial buyers such as mining companies and data centres are natural anchor off-takers because they need 24-hour reliable power.
04
Build-Own-Operate Models
Some vendor countries, particularly Russia and China, offer Build-Own-Operate financing where the vendor finances, builds, owns, and operates the plant, with the host country paying per kilowatt-hour over decades. This reduces upfront risk for the host country but creates long-term strategic dependencies.
01
The Energy Deficit
600 million Africans have no access to electricity. Africa's total electricity generation is expected to grow sevenfold by 2050. The continent produces 14 percent of global uranium supply from Namibia, Niger, and South Africa. The resources, the need, and the technology are converging.
02
The SMR Window
SMR technology is reaching commercial readiness at exactly the moment when African energy demand is accelerating. Countries that move now on regulatory preparation, workforce development, and site selection will be positioned to deploy in the early 2030s. Countries that wait face a 10 to 15 year delay from standing start.
03
Industrial Demand as Anchor
Africa's mining sector, growing data centre industry, and manufacturing zones need 24-hour baseload power that solar and wind cannot reliably provide. These industrial consumers are natural anchor buyers for nuclear power. Their PPAs provide the revenue certainty that unlocks financing.
04
The Rwanda Proof-of-Concept
A successful Rwandan SMR deployment would be the first reference plant in a small African grid context. This reduces technology risk perception for every subsequent African program. The commercial and geopolitical value of being first is enormous. Rwanda is not just building a power plant; it is building the template.

LEGAL / ADVISORY / TRANSACTIONAL FRAMEWORKS / NUCLEAR ENERGY PROJECTS
LEGAL AND ADVISORY
Nuclear energy projects are among the most legally complex infrastructure transactions in the world. This module maps the full legal and transactional architecture from national law enactment through technology vendor selection, financial close, and construction contracts. Every layer must be in place before a nuclear project can achieve bankability.
COUNSEL
Experienced legal counsel is essential to proper structuring and risk allocation in nuclear transaction documents. The complexity of nuclear law, international liability conventions, EPC structures, and PPA mechanics requires specialised expertise that Rwanda and all newcomer countries must secure early in Phase 2.
National Legal Architecture
FOUNDATIONAL LAW
Comprehensive Nuclear Energy Law
The nuclear law is the legal foundation for the entire programme. It must establish the independent regulatory body, define licensing requirements, set safety and radiation protection standards, govern nuclear waste and decommissioning obligations, establish nuclear liability provisions, and create the legal framework for safeguards implementation. Rwanda's nuclear law was drafted in 2025 and is under legislative review as the priority item identified in the IAEA INIR Phase 1 mission.
PRIORITY ACTIONPHASE 2 GATE
REGULATORY FRAMEWORK
Licensing and Regulatory Policy
Rwanda must develop a complete regulatory framework covering site licensing, construction permits, operating licences, and decommissioning licences. The framework must align with IAEA Safety Standards and the requirements of Milestone 2 of the IAEA Milestones Approach. RAEB serves as the independent regulatory body and must develop the technical competency and legal instruments to exercise its licensing authority. Regulatory inspectors and technical staff capacity is an immediate development priority.
IN PROGRESS
INTERNATIONAL CONVENTIONS
Nuclear Liability Framework
Nuclear liability is governed by international conventions including the Vienna Convention on Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage and the Paris Convention. These instruments establish the principle of strict, exclusive, and limited liability of the operator. Accession to these conventions is typically required by project finance lenders as a condition of financing. Without a clear national liability framework aligned to international conventions, nuclear project finance cannot be structured. Rwanda must complete this legal instrument as part of Phase 2 preparation.
REQUIRED FOR FINANCE
SAFEGUARDS
IAEA Safeguards Agreements
Rwanda must maintain a Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement with the IAEA and is strongly encouraged to sign the Additional Protocol, which provides enhanced inspection rights. The Additional Protocol is a de facto requirement for vendor technology agreements and international financing. No credible nuclear technology vendor will proceed with a legally binding agreement with a country that has not signed the Additional Protocol. This is a non-negotiable element of the international legal architecture for nuclear programmes.
CRITICAL PATH
Transaction Architecture for Nuclear Projects
INTERGOVERNMENTAL AGREEMENT
Host Government Agreement and IGA
Intergovernmental Agreements establish the bilateral framework between the host country and the technology-supplying country. They cover technology transfer terms, financing arrangements, training commitments, waste management obligations, and sovereignty protections. Rwanda has executed an IGA with Russia for the Centre for Nuclear Science and Technology. Future IGAs for SMR deployment will be more complex, covering construction, fuel supply, operations support, and decommissioning across a 60 to 80 year programme lifecycle.
EXAMPLE: RUSSIA IGA 2019
EPC CONTRACT STRUCTURE
Engineering, Procurement and Construction
Nuclear EPC contracts are typically structured on a fixed-price, lump-sum turnkey basis to provide construction cost certainty for project finance lenders. The EPC contractor bears construction risk against a guaranteed completion date and performance obligations. For SMR projects, the EPC scope typically includes nuclear island construction, balance of plant, turbine hall, grid connection, and site preparation. Rwanda's EPC contract will be one of the most significant procurement actions in its history and requires specialist legal and owner engineering support.
PHASE 3 ACTION
POWER PURCHASE AGREEMENT
PPA Structure and Creditworthiness
The Power Purchase Agreement is the revenue contract that underpins project finance. A bankable nuclear PPA must be denominated in a hard currency or include adequate foreign exchange protections, have a term of at least 20 to 25 years matching the debt repayment period, include a creditworthy off-taker (government utility, industrial anchor or a combination), specify capacity payment structures that ensure debt service coverage regardless of dispatch, and include regulatory change protections. Multiple anchor off-takers from Rwanda's mining, data centre, and manufacturing sectors strengthen the credit structure.
BANKABILITY GATE
TECHNOLOGY LICENSING
Vendor Technology and Fuel Supply Agreements
Technology licensing agreements with the selected SMR vendor govern intellectual property rights, proprietary design information access, fuel supply arrangements, spare parts, technical support, and operator training. These agreements must be structured carefully to avoid dependency and to protect Rwanda's long-term operational sovereignty. Fuel supply agreements are particularly important given that uranium enrichment is controlled by a small number of suppliers globally, and supply security must be contractually guaranteed across the operational lifetime of the plant.

MODEL
Catalytic Capital: Public Interest and Early Stage Capacity
PRINCIPLE
Catalytic capital accepts below-market returns or higher risk to generate social, environmental, or public interest outcomes that commercial markets cannot deliver alone. For nuclear energy in Africa, catalytic capital plays three roles: funding the pre-development phase that no commercial investor will finance, providing first-loss equity that absorbs construction risk and enables senior debt, and demonstrating that nuclear energy projects in African markets are viable investments, permanently reducing the risk premium for future transactions.
Public Interest Rationale: The Case for Concessional Capital in Nuclear
Six hundred million Africans without electricity is the defining public interest case. The UNECA Nuclear Energy Innovation Summit for Africa in Kigali in July 2025 concluded that a viable path forward requires leveraging international development finance for de-risking, reorienting domestic financial sectors toward long-term infrastructure, structuring innovative blended finance and PPPs, and strategically aligning nuclear projects with global climate goals. These are the four conditions that catalytic capital must satisfy simultaneously.
VALIDATED
Early Stage Capacity: Pre-Development Grant Facility (Proposed)
A dedicated Nuclear Pre-Development Facility for Africa would provide grants and concessional loans for feasibility studies, owner engineering, regulatory capacity, legal framework development, and site characterisation for countries with completed IAEA Phase 1 INIR missions. The facility would be funded by DFIs, climate funds, and nuclear-technology countries as a public interest catalytic instrument. Rwanda, Ghana, and Nigeria are the immediate eligible beneficiaries given their current programme stages. The financial requirement is USD 20 to 80 million per country over 3 to 5 years.
PROPOSED
SCALED: Scaling Capital for Sustainable Development
SCALED (formerly HSP) is a joint World Bank and G20 initiative that standardises financial vehicles and pools funds from public donors to scale sustainable investments in emerging markets and developing economies. It makes it easier for private investors to deploy capital by standardising structures. SCALED represents a potential channel for nuclear infrastructure financing as the World Bank's nuclear energy policy evolves, particularly for SMR deployments in countries with completed IAEA milestone reviews.
POLICY TRACK
First-Loss Equity Structure for African Nuclear
DFI concessional capital at the mezzanine level absorbing first-loss risk provides credit enhancement and improves debt service coverage ratios, making projects more attractive to senior lenders. The base rate can account for 60 to 90% of the cost of capital in African infrastructure projects. Sovereign risk is the dominant driver. DFI first-loss equity placed below commercial senior debt directly addresses this constraint, reducing the cost of capital and improving project bankability. East Africa is forecast to record the highest regional growth on the continent at 5.8% in 2026, strengthening the macroeconomic case for Rwanda-anchored nuclear financing.

WHY THIS EXISTS

Nuclear science capability cannot be imported indefinitely. It has to be cultivated. The data and formalism inside IAEA-TECDOC-1506 underpin how every functioning nuclear programme, research reactor, medical isotope facility, and regulatory body in the world models nuclear reactions: what a neutron does when it strikes a nucleus, how an excited nucleus releases energy, how a fission event unfolds, and how confidently any of that can be predicted.

This classroom exists so that capability is not locked inside a 172-page reference document accessible only to specialists who already know where to look. Every chapter is rebuilt as a module: the physics explained in plain language first, then in full mathematical form, then made interactive so a learner can change a parameter and watch the physics respond, then connected explicitly to what Rwanda and the region need it for.

Atomic Masses & Ground State Properties
Every nucleus has a mass slightly less than the sum of its separate protons and neutrons. That missing mass is the binding energy, the single number that determines whether splitting or joining nuclei releases energy, and by how much.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

Take a nucleus apart into its individual protons and neutrons, weigh the pieces separately, and weigh the whole nucleus. The whole nucleus weighs less. That missing mass did not vanish: Einstein's relation converted it into the binding energy holding the nucleus together. The more tightly bound a nucleus, the lower its mass relative to its separated parts, and the more energy it would take to pull it apart.

This single fact explains both ends of nuclear energy. Fission releases energy because splitting a heavy nucleus into two medium nuclei increases the total binding energy per particle. Fusion releases energy because joining two very light nuclei does the same thing from the other direction. Iron-56 sits near the peak of binding energy per nucleon, which is why elements lighter than iron release energy by fusing, and elements heavier than iron release energy by splitting.

BINDING ENERGY
Nuclear Binding Energy
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 2.1
B(Z,N) = [ Z·MH + N·Mn − M(Z,N) ]·c2
The binding energy B is the energy equivalent of the mass difference between the separated nucleons and the bound nucleus. Z protons (taken as hydrogen atom mass to include the orbital electron bookkeeping) and N neutrons, weighed apart, always outweigh the same nucleons bound together as a nucleus of mass M(Z,N).

Binding Energy per Nucleon
Foundation & Research
DERIVED
B/A = B(Z,N) / (Z + N)
This is the number that actually predicts whether fission or fusion releases energy for a given nucleus. It rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks around iron and nickel (A ≈ 56–62, roughly 8.7–8.8 MeV per nucleon), and declines slowly through the heavy actinides. Any process moving a nucleus toward the peak releases energy.
Semi-Empirical Mass Formula (Bethe-Weizsacker form)
Research

B = avA − asA2/3 − acZ(Z−1)A−1/3 − aa(N−Z)2A−1 + δ(A,Z)
Five physical effects, summed. The volume term scales with A because binding is mostly a short-range, saturating force. The surface term subtracts energy because nucleons at the surface have fewer neighbours. The Coulomb term subtracts energy because protons repel each other electrostatically. The asymmetry term subtracts energy when N and Z are unequal, reflecting the Pauli exclusion principle filling separate proton and neutron levels. The pairing term δ adds extra binding when protons or neutrons pair up.

The smooth liquid-drop formula above misses shell effects: real nuclei with magic numbers of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) are bound noticeably tighter than the smooth formula predicts. Discrete Levels & Decay
A nucleus can absorb energy and become excited, exactly like an atom's electrons jumping to a higher orbital, except the energy steps are millions of times larger and the structure far richer. Mapping these discrete energy levels, and how a nucleus falls back down through them, is the foundation of every isotope production and detection scheme.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

Just below a certain excitation energy, a nucleus can only exist in a specific, countable set of energy levels, each with a definite spin and parity, the same way a guitar string can only vibrate at its fundamental frequency and exact harmonics. Above that energy, called the completeness energy Emax, levels become so dense they blur into a statistical continuum (this is where Module M5, level densities, takes over).

When a nucleus is sitting in an excited level, it does not stay there. It falls toward the ground state by emitting gamma rays, by internal conversion (kicking out an orbital electron instead), or eventually by particle or radioactive decay. The pattern of which levels a nucleus falls through, and how often it takes each path, is the decay scheme, and it is exactly what a gamma-ray detector measures in a medical isotope facility or a research laboratory.

COMPLETENESS & THE DISCRETE-TO-CONTINUUM TRANSITION
Completeness Energy Emax
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 SEC. 3.1
Below Emax, spectroscopists are confident every level has been found, and every level's energy, spin, and parity is known. Above it, levels are too dense and too weakly populated by ordinary experiments to resolve individually, so reaction calculations switch from a discrete level list to a statistical level density function. RIPL-2's Discrete Level Scheme Library (DLSL) gives Emax for a large number of nuclei, determined by where the observed level count starts deviating from the smooth statistical trend.
Constant Temperature Level Number
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 SEC. 3.1.3
N(U) = exp[(U − U0) / T] − 1
A simple, remarkably effective fit to the cumulative number of known levels below excitation energy U. The fitted "nuclear temperature" T describes how rapidly levels pile up with energy, and the same T parameter reappears as the low-energy anchor for level density models in Module M5.

Internal conversion coefficients (ICC)
Not every excited nucleus emits its decay energy as a gamma ray. Some of the time, the electromagnetic field directly ejects an orbital electron instead, a process called internal conversion. The internal conversion coefficient is the ratio of conversion-electron emission probability to gamma-ray emission probability for a given transition, and RIPL-2 calculates these wherever ENSDF measurements are missing, because the actual detected radiation type changes if conversion electrons rather than gammas are escaping the source.

APPLICATION: DECAY SCHEMES IN ISOTOPE PRODUCTION

When a target is irradiated to produce a medical isotope, the product nucleus is usually born in an excited state, not directly in its ground state. It cascades down through a sequence of discrete levels, emitting a characteristic pattern of gamma rays along the way. That pattern is the basis for two practical needs: identifying the isotope by its gamma-ray "fingerprint," and predicting whether a useful fraction of decays pass through a long-lived isomeric state that affects how the isotope must be stored, shipped, and used.

Branching ratios, the probability of taking one decay path over another, determine the practical yield of a desired isotope versus competing decay products, which is precisely the kind of calculation an isotope production programme needs before committing reactor or accelerator time to an irradiation run.

Neutron Resonance Parameters
Fire neutrons at a nucleus across a range of energies and the absorption probability does not rise smoothly. It spikes sharply at specific energies, called resonances, where the neutron energy plus the nucleus exactly matches an excited state. Reactor physics runs on understanding these spikes.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

A resonance is a coincidence of energy. When an incoming neutron's kinetic energy, added to the binding energy it would gain by joining the nucleus, happens to match a real excited state of the resulting nucleus, the capture probability shoots up by orders of magnitude. Between resonances, the cross section drops back to a smooth, low background. The result, plotted against neutron energy, is a forest of sharp peaks.

This matters enormously for reactor design. As a reactor heats up, the resonances broaden slightly (Doppler broadening) which changes how much neutron absorption happens at each resonance energy. This is one of the principal physical effects giving reactors a stabilizing negative feedback: a temperature rise increases resonance absorption, which removes neutrons from the chain reaction, which slows the reaction down.

NEUTRON STRENGTH FUNCTION
Neutron Strength Function Sl
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 4.1
Sl = ⟨gΓln⟩ / [(2l+1)Dl] = (1 / [(2l+1)ΔE]) · Σr grΓlnr
The strength function averages the neutron-capture widths of many resonances of orbital angular momentum l over an energy interval ΔE, normalizing out how closely spaced they happen to be. It is one of the three core quantities, alongside resonance spacing and radiative width, that a statistical reaction calculation needs as input, and it is what lets a single averaged number stand in for hundreds of individual resonance measurements.

Average Resonance Spacing
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 4.2
Dl = ΔE / (N − 1)
The simplest possible definition: the energy window divided by the number of resonance gaps within it. In practice this is the hard quantity to evaluate accurately, because experiments miss weak resonances, which makes the measured spacing look larger than the true spacing. Most of Chapter 4's methodology exists to correct for exactly this bias.
Statistical Precision & the Missing-Resonance Problem
Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 4.3 – 4.4
δSl / Sl = √(2/N)      δDl / Dl ≈ (0.45√(ln N) + 2.18) / N
The strength function's relative error falls off as 1/√N (Porter-Thomas statistics), but the spacing's relative error from the Dyson-Mehta treatment of the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble falls off closer to 1/N, which sounds favourable until missing resonances are accounted for: any unobserved weak resonance inflates the apparent spacing far more than this formula alone implies. RIPL-2 documents only U-235, U-238, Pu-239, Pu-240 and Pu-242 as having resonance spacings known to better than 5–10%, which is itself a measure of how difficult this measurement is.
Porter-Thomas Width Distribution
Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 4.7
N(Γ) = N0[1 − erf(√(Γ/2Γ̅))]
Because a resonance width is built from a random superposition of many nuclear configurations, the reduced neutron widths across a set of resonances follow a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom, the Porter-Thomas distribution. Crucially, this distribution's upper tail (the strong resonances) is barely affected even when weak resonances are missed entirely, which is why fitting the strong-resonance tail is a more robust way to extract the true average width than simply averaging everything observed.

APPLICATION: REACTOR TEMPERATURE FEEDBACK

Doppler broadening of neutron resonances is one of the most important inherent safety features in reactor physics. As fuel temperature rises, nuclei vibrate more vigorously, which smears each resonance peak over a wider range of effective neutron energies as seen by a neutron in the surrounding medium. A wider, shorter peak captures slightly more neutrons in total than a narrow, tall one (the resonance integral increases), which removes more neutrons from the chain reaction and provides a fast, inherent, physics-based brake on reactivity, well before any mechanical control system reacts.

This is precisely why accurate resonance parameters for fuel and fertile isotopes such as U-235, U-238, and Pu-239 are essential reactor design inputs: they determine the magnitude of this safety margin quantitatively, not just qualitatively.

Optical Model & Nuclear Reactions
A nucleus reacts with an incoming particle the way a cloudy lens reacts with light: it absorbs some of it and bends the rest. The optical model treats the nucleus as exactly that, a partially absorbing, partially refracting object, and from that single idea, a remarkable range of reaction probabilities can be calculated.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

Tracking every individual nucleon's interaction with an incoming particle is computationally hopeless for any nucleus with more than a few particles. The optical model sidesteps this by replacing the real, complicated many-body nucleus with a single smooth average potential well, the same trick used in optics to describe how light bends and is absorbed inside a piece of cloudy glass without tracking every individual photon-electron collision.

This average potential has both a real part, which bends the incoming particle's trajectory (refraction), and an imaginary part, which removes the particle from the beam by exciting the nucleus into other outcomes (absorption). From this one potential, calculated once for a given nucleus and incident particle, the model predicts a whole family of practical numbers: how much of the beam is scattered straight through, how much is absorbed, and crucially, the transmission coefficients that the Hauser-Feshbach formula needs to predict the probability of every individual reaction outcome.

THE OPTICAL POTENTIAL
General Optical Model Potential
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 5.1
V(r) = −VRfR(r) − iWVfV(r) + 4aVDVDd/dr fVD(r) + 4iaWDWDd/dr fWD(r) + [spin−orbit terms]
Four physical pieces, each multiplying a radial form factor fi(r) that describes how that piece of the potential is distributed in space. The real volume term VR handles refraction. The imaginary volume term WV handles absorption into the compound nucleus. The surface derivative terms (real VD and imaginary WD) concentrate their effect at the nuclear surface, where direct, fast reaction processes dominate. A spin-orbit term (not shown in full here) couples the particle's spin to its orbital motion.
V_R, W_Vreal/imaginary volume well depths
V_D, W_Dreal/imaginary surface well depths

Woods-Saxon Form Factor
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 5.5
fi(r) = 1 / { 1 + exp[ (r − Ri) / ai ] }
This is the standard shape for the nuclear potential well: roughly flat (equal to −1) deep inside the nucleus, falling smoothly to zero outside it, with the falloff centred at radius Ri = riA1/3 and a diffuseness ai describing how gradually the nuclear surface fades rather than ending sharply. It is the same functional form that appears throughout nuclear physics wherever a smooth, saturating density profile is needed.
FROM POTENTIAL TO CROSS SECTION
Hauser-Feshbach Cross Section
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 SEC. 1, INTRO
σa→b = ΣJπ TaTbΣiΣcTic
This is the formula that turns optical model transmission coefficients into an actual reaction probability. The compound nucleus, once formed, has no memory of how it was formed; it simply decays into whichever exit channel is available, weighted by that channel's transmission coefficient T, calculated by the optical model. The denominator sums transmission coefficients over every open channel i and every accessible final state c within it, discrete levels at low energy, a continuum described by the level density (Module M5) at higher energy, so the cross section for any specific outcome is simply its share of the total decay probability.

Two regimes: individual potentials and global potentials
RIPL-2's optical model parameter library distinguishes individual nucleus potentials, fitted to one target or a narrow range for maximum accuracy, from global potentials, fitted across a wide span of Z, A, and incident energy so they can be applied to nuclei with no dedicated measurement. The most widely used global potential for both neutrons and protons is Koning and Delaroche (Z = 12–83, A = 27–209, E = 0.001–200 MeV); the Madland potential extends global coverage to higher energy (E = 50–400 MeV).

Nuclear Level Densities
Above a few MeV of excitation energy, a nucleus has so many possible internal configurations that individual levels can no longer be counted, only described statistically. The level density ρ(U) is the function that says how many levels per unit energy exist at excitation energy U, and it is the single most consequential statistical quantity in all of nuclear reaction theory.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

Imagine a nucleus as a container of protons and neutrons sitting in quantum energy shells, like a stadium with seats arranged in tiers. At low excitation energy, only a few particles can be promoted to higher seats in a few possible ways, so there are only a handful of distinct excited configurations, the discrete levels of Module M2. As excitation energy rises, exponentially more combinations of which particles sit in which seats become possible. The number of distinct configurations per unit energy, the level density, grows roughly exponentially with the square root of excitation energy.

This single function is the hidden engine behind the Hauser-Feshbach cross section formula from Module M4. Whenever a compound nucleus has a continuum of possible final states rather than a short list of known discrete levels, it is the level density that tells the calculation how many ways the nucleus can land, and therefore how the available reaction probability gets divided among gamma emission, neutron emission, fission, and every other open channel. Get the level density wrong, and every downstream prediction, reactor cross section, isotope yield, fission rate, inherits that error.

THE FERMI GAS MODEL
Bethe Fermi Gas Level Density
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 6.1
ρ(U) = √π12a1/4U5/4 exp(2√(aU))
This is the foundational result: treat the nucleus as a gas of independent fermions filling energy levels up to a Fermi surface, count the ways to distribute excitation energy U among them, and the level density grows as exp(2√aU), an exponential in the square root of energy, the signature shape of any system with this kind of combinatorial level counting. The level density parameter a sets how fast this growth happens, and is proportional to the density of single-particle states near the Fermi surface.

Total level density alone is not enough; reaction calculations need to know how many levels exist at each spin J, because angular momentum is conserved in every reaction step. RIPL-2 EQ. 6.6
Even-even nuclei (paired protons and paired neutrons) are systematically harder to excite than odd or odd-odd nuclei, the same pairing effect seen in binding energies (Module M1). a-Parameter Systematics (Shell-Corrected)
Research

This is precisely how a property from the mass table propagates all the way through to a reaction rate calculation.

APPLICATION: MEDICAL ISOTOPE YIELD PREDICTION

Predicting how much of a desired medical isotope an irradiation will produce requires multiplying a reaction cross section (Module M4, Hauser-Feshbach) by a flux and an irradiation time, but that cross section calculation itself needs the level density of the product nucleus at every relevant excitation energy to know how the reaction probability is distributed among all the ways the compound nucleus can decay. A level density that is too high or too low at the energies relevant to producing, for example, Mo-99 for Tc-99m generators, directly mispredicts production yield, and therefore mispredicts how much reactor or accelerator time is needed to meet a clinical supply target.

Gamma-Ray Strength Functions
Almost every nuclear reaction ends with the nucleus shedding its remaining excitation as gamma rays. The gamma-ray strength function describes how readily a nucleus emits, or absorbs, a photon of a given energy, and it directly controls capture cross sections and isomer production, both central to medical isotope planning.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

Picture the protons inside a nucleus, collectively, sloshing back and forth against the neutrons, like two interpenetrating fluids oscillating out of phase. This collective oscillation is the Giant Dipole Resonance (GDR), and it is the dominant mechanism by which a nucleus absorbs or emits a gamma ray. The gamma-ray strength function is essentially a map of how strongly this oscillation can be driven at every possible photon energy, peaking sharply at the GDR's natural resonance energy and falling away on either side.

This single function appears twice, in mirror-image roles. Run it "upward," and it predicts how strongly a nucleus absorbs an incoming gamma ray, the basis of photo-nuclear reactions. Run it "downward," and it predicts how readily an excited nucleus emits a gamma ray of a given energy on its way back to the ground state, which is exactly the calculation needed to predict capture cross sections and the gamma cascades that determine which isomeric state a medical isotope production reaction populates.

THE TWO STRENGTH FUNCTIONS
Downward (Emission) Strength Function
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 7.1
f↓XL(εγ) = ⟨ΓXL(εγ)⟩ / [εγ2L+1D]
The strength function a nucleus uses to emit a gamma ray of energy εγ while de-exciting: the average reduced radiative width of that multipole transition, normalized by the resonance spacing D. This is what determines the transmission coefficient for gamma emission in the Hauser-Feshbach formula of Module M4.
Upward (Photo-Absorption) Strength Function
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 7.2
f↑E1(εγ) = εγ−1σE1(εγ) / [3(πħc)2]
The mirror image, derived from the measured photo-absorption cross section σ: how strongly a nucleus absorbs an incoming gamma ray of energy εγ. For E1 transitions specifically, this is dominated entirely by the Giant Dipole Resonance.
MODELLING THE GIANT DIPOLE RESONANCE
Standard Lorentzian (SLO / Brink-Axel)
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 7.4
f↓SLO(εγ) = 8.674×10−8 σrΓr εγΓr(εγ2−Er2)2+(εγΓr)2   [MeV−3]
The simplest, most widely used model: a plain Lorentzian peak centred at the GDR energy Er, with peak cross section σr and width Γr, all three taken directly from photo-absorption measurements. It works well for medium and heavy nuclei in the resonance region itself, but systematically underestimates emission at low gamma-ray energies (below roughly 1 MeV), which matters because low-energy transitions are common in the final steps of a decay cascade.

APPLICATION: CAPTURE CROSS SECTIONS & ISOMER PRODUCTION

The gamma-ray strength function sets the transmission coefficient for radiative capture, TXL(εγ) = 2πεγ2L+1f↓XL(εγ), which plugs directly into the Hauser-Feshbach formula of Module M4 wherever radiative capture competes with particle emission as a decay channel. Nuclear Fission
Every module so far has been building toward this one. Fission is the process every Module M1 through M6 quantity ultimately serves to predict: when, how readily, and with what energy release a heavy nucleus splits.
THE CENTRAL IDEA

Picture a heavy nucleus as a drop of charged liquid. Surface tension wants to hold it together in a sphere; electrical repulsion between all those protons wants to tear it apart. For most nuclei, surface tension wins comfortably. But for the heaviest nuclei, the two forces are close enough in strength that stretching the drop into a dumbbell shape, then pinching it into two separate drops, costs only a modest amount of energy. That modest energy cost is the fission barrier, and the entire predictability of nuclear fission, in a reactor, in a weapon, or in nature, comes down to how high that barrier is and how easily it can be crossed.

Once a nucleus does cross that barrier and splits, it does not split evenly. Mass distributes asymmetrically between the two fragments, both fragments fly apart at high speed, and the missing binding energy (from Module M1) is released as kinetic energy, neutrons, and gamma rays. That energy release, multiplied across the roughly 1019 fission events happening every second in an operating power reactor, is the entire physical basis of nuclear energy.

THE FISSION BARRIER
Fission Barrier (Liquid Drop + Shell Correction)
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 8.3
Bf = Bld − δE0 + δEf
The actual fission barrier height has three pieces. Bld is the smooth liquid-drop prediction. δE0 is the ground-state shell correction (the same correction that makes real masses deviate from the smooth mass formula in Module M1); subtracting it raises the effective barrier for nuclei with extra ground-state binding. δEf is the shell correction at the elongated saddle-point shape itself, usually small or vanishing for the strongly deformed shapes pre-actinide nuclei pass through, but significant for the actinides.
Liquid-Drop Barrier (Thomas-Fermi Fit)
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 8.4 – 8.5
Bld(Z,N) = S(Z,N)·F(X)      X = Z2/[A(1−kI2)]
The fissility parameter X is, in essence, the ratio of Coulomb repulsion energy to surface tension energy, exactly the competition described conceptually above. The closer X climbs toward its critical value (around 46–48), the lower the barrier, and the more readily the nucleus fissions. This single number is why uranium and plutonium are fissile while iron is not: it is purely a question of how many protons are crowded into the nucleus relative to its surface area.

THE TWO-HUMPED BARRIER (ACTINIDES)
Why Actinide Barriers Have Two Peaks
Foundation & Research
RIPL-2 SEC. 8.2
For actinide nuclei, shell corrections do not vanish at the saddle point the way they do for pre-actinides. Instead, the deformation energy curve develops a deep secondary well partway through the fission path, producing a barrier with two humps (commonly labelled A, the inner hump, and B, the outer hump) rather than one smooth peak. This was not a theoretical prediction confirmed later; it was discovered because it was the only explanation for two otherwise baffling experimental observations: spontaneously-fissioning isomers of americium with anomalously short half-lives, and fine resonance structure in neutron-induced fission cross sections that a single smooth barrier could never produce. The nucleus, it turned out, was pausing in that secondary well on its way to splitting.
Fission Width & Barrier Penetrability
Research
RIPL-2 EQ. 8.1, 8.2, 8.9
Γf(Uc) = [Dc(Uc)/2π] ΣTfi      Ti(E) = { 1 + exp[(2π/ωi)(Ei−E)] }−1
The fission width, exactly like a particle-emission width, is the level spacing times a sum of transmission coefficients, only here the channels are the barrier humps rather than outgoing particles. Each hump's penetrability follows the standard parabolic-barrier tunnelling formula, where Ei is the hump's transition-state energy and ωi is its curvature (a sharper, narrower hump is easier to tunnel through than a broad one of the same height). For a two-humped barrier with humps A and B, transmission through both in sequence, modulated by whether the nucleus gets trapped decaying gamma-ray-style inside the secondary well, determines the overall fission probability.
Fission level densities are not the same as ground-state level densities
The saddle-point configuration is a strongly deformed shape, very different from the compact ground state, so the level density at the saddle point, ρf in the fission-width formula, generally needs its own a-parameter and pairing treatment, distinct from the ground-state level density of Module M5. RIPL-2 provides this paired set of fission barrier and saddle-point level density parameters precisely because the two are so strongly coupled in any actual fission-probability calculation.

APPLICATION: SMR FUEL CYCLE & ENERGY PLANNING

Every quantity this classroom has covered converges here. The binding energy difference between U-235 and its fission fragments (Module M1) sets the energy released per fission. Neutron resonance behaviour (Module M3) and optical-model cross sections (Module M4) set how efficiently a reactor design captures neutrons to sustain the chain reaction. Level densities (Module M5) and gamma strength functions (Module M6) determine how excitation energy is distributed and radiated. The fission barrier itself determines which isotopes are usable reactor fuel at all, and how a reactor's fuel cycle must be designed around fissile versus fertile material.

For small modular reactor (SMR) planning specifically, fission barrier and yield data for the candidate fuel form, whether conventional low-enriched uranium or an alternative fuel cycle, are a foundational input to every subsequent core physics, safety, and fuel cycle economics calculation.

Glossary & Symbols
Core terms and symbols used throughout the seven modules, gathered in one place for quick lookup.
TERMS
Binding Energy (B)
The energy equivalent of the mass difference between a nucleus and its separated constituent protons and neutrons; the energy required to fully disassemble the nucleus.
Q-value
The net energy released or absorbed in a nuclear reaction, equal to the binding energy of the products minus the binding energy of the reactants.
Discrete Level / Completeness Energy (E_max)
Below E_max, individual excited states of a nucleus are fully catalogued by energy, spin, and parity. Above it, levels are treated statistically.
Internal Conversion
A decay process where an excited nucleus transfers its energy directly to an orbital electron, ejecting it, instead of emitting a gamma ray.
Resonance
A sharp peak in reaction probability occurring when the incoming particle energy plus binding energy matches an excited state of the resulting nucleus exactly.
Neutron Strength Function (S_l)
The average reduced neutron width per unit energy interval for resonances of orbital angular momentum l, normalizing many individual resonance measurements into one statistical quantity.
Doppler Broadening
The widening of resonance peaks with increasing nuclear temperature due to thermal motion, a key inherent reactor safety feedback mechanism.
Optical Model
A model treating the nucleus as a smooth, partially absorbing average potential well for the purpose of calculating reaction probabilities with an incoming particle.
Transmission Coefficient (T)
The probability, calculated by the optical model, that a given reaction channel is taken; the core input to the Hauser-Feshbach formula.
Hauser-Feshbach Theory
A statistical framework for calculating nuclear reaction cross sections by assuming the compound nucleus decays with no memory of how it was formed, weighted purely by channel transmission coefficients.
Level Density (ρ)
The number of nuclear energy levels per unit energy interval at a given excitation energy, growing roughly as exp(2√aU) in the Fermi gas model.
Level Density Parameter (a)
A nucleus-specific constant controlling how rapidly level density grows with excitation energy, proportional to the single-particle state density near the Fermi surface.
Spin Cut-off Parameter (σ)
A parameter describing how level density is distributed across different spin values at a given excitation energy.
Giant Dipole Resonance (GDR)
A collective oscillation of protons against neutrons within a nucleus, the dominant mechanism for gamma-ray absorption and emission.
Gamma-Ray Strength Function (f)
A function describing how strongly a nucleus emits or absorbs a photon of a given energy, central to capture cross sections and isomer production.
Fission Barrier (B_f)
The energy cost of deforming a nucleus from its ground-state shape to the elongated saddle-point shape that precedes splitting into two fragments.
Fissility Parameter (X)
A dimensionless measure, proportional to Z²/A, of the competition between Coulomb repulsion and surface tension; determines how readily a nucleus fissions.
Two-Humped Fission Barrier
The double-peaked deformation energy curve found in actinide nuclei, with a secondary potential well between the humps that hosts fission isomers.
Compound Nucleus
The temporary, fully-equilibrated excited nucleus formed immediately after a particle is absorbed, before it decays through any available channel.
