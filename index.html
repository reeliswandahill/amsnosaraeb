In December 2020, Rwanda made a decisive commitment. Presidential Order 127/01 formally established the Rwanda Atomic Energy Board, the institutional engine of a national nuclear programme. President Kagame's framing was direct: nuclear is the platform for industrial growth, not just a power source.

EDUCATION / INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERS
LEARNING HUB
For students, educators, policymakers, and any first-time visitor. Plain-language explainers on nuclear energy, the IAEA process, financing structures, and why Rwanda's approach is distinctive.
01
Fission: The Core Process
Nuclear energy is produced by splitting uranium atoms in a controlled process called fission. When a uranium-235 atom absorbs a neutron, it splits into smaller atoms and releases a large amount of energy as heat. No combustion occurs, so there are no carbon dioxide emissions during operation.
02
Heat to Electricity
The heat from fission is used to produce steam. Steam drives a turbine connected to a generator, which produces electricity. This is the same process used in coal and gas plants, but the fuel source is nuclear rather than fossil fuels.
03
The Chain Reaction
Each fission event releases additional neutrons, which can split more uranium atoms. This self-sustaining chain reaction is carefully controlled by inserting or withdrawing control rods. A nuclear plant cannot explode like a nuclear bomb because the fuel is not enriched to the required level.
04
Waste Volume
The volume of high-level nuclear waste produced is remarkably small. All the spent fuel ever produced by US commercial nuclear reactors would fit inside a single Walmart. The waste is solid, dense, and can be safely stored in sealed containers for very long periods.
01
What is an SMR
A Small Modular Reactor generates between 10 and 300 MW of electricity, compared to 1,000 MW or more for a conventional large reactor. SMRs are built in factories and transported to site, reducing construction time and cost. Rwanda's target is units of 110 to 150 MW per module.
02
Why SMRs Suit Africa
Africa's electricity grids are smaller than those in Europe or North America. A 1,000 MW reactor would overwhelm most African grids. SMRs can be scaled incrementally, adding capacity as demand grows. They require less water and have smaller site footprints, making them suitable for landlocked countries like Rwanda.
03
Passive Safety
Most SMR designs feature passive safety systems. In an emergency, cooling happens automatically through physics: gravity, natural circulation, and heat transfer, without needing electricity or human action. The reactor shuts down safely on its own, unlike older reactor designs.
04
Financing Advantage
A 77 MW SMR module might cost USD 200 to 300 million, compared to USD 10 billion or more for a large conventional reactor. This lower upfront cost makes financing more accessible. Countries can also stage investment, adding modules as revenue from early units begins to flow.
01
The Milestones Approach
The IAEA provides a structured three-phase framework for countries developing nuclear programmes. Phase 1 is Consider: readiness to commit. Phase 2 is Prepare: ready to invite bids. Phase 3 is Construct: ready to commission. Rwanda has completed Phase 1.
02
The INIR Mission
An Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review is a formal assessment conducted by international experts appointed by the IAEA. They evaluate the country across 19 specific infrastructure areas including safety, security, safeguards, legal framework, waste management, and public information. Rwanda's INIR took place 2 to 9 March 2026.
03
The 19 Infrastructure Areas
These include: national position, nuclear safety, management, funding and financing, legal framework, safeguards, regulatory framework, radiation protection, electrical grid, human resource development, stakeholder involvement, site and supporting facilities, environmental protection, emergency planning, nuclear security, nuclear fuel cycle, radioactive waste management, industrial involvement, and procurement.
04
What Good Practices Mean
When the IAEA identifies a good practice, it means Rwanda's approach in that area is considered a model that other countries should study and replicate. The March 2026 mission identified Rwanda's government coordination, stakeholder engagement, and emergency preparedness as good practices. This is a significant institutional endorsement.
01
The Financing Challenge
Nuclear plants have high upfront capital costs, typically 70 to 85 percent fixed before a single kilowatt-hour is generated. This front-loaded cost profile is the primary barrier for developing countries. SMRs reduce this barrier significantly, with a 100 MW unit estimated at USD 200 to 300 million vs USD 5 to 10 billion for a large reactor.
02
Blended Finance
Most nuclear projects in emerging markets use blended finance: a combination of government equity, development finance institution (DFI) loans, export credit agency (ECA) guarantees from the vendor country, and commercial debt. Each layer de-risks the investment for the next. Rwanda's model will likely follow this structure.
03
Power Purchase Agreements
A PPA is a long-term contract between the plant owner and an electricity buyer, typically 20 to 30 years. PPAs provide revenue certainty, which enables lenders to commit financing. Industrial buyers such as mining companies and data centres are natural anchor off-takers because they need 24-hour reliable power.
04
Build-Own-Operate Models
Some vendor countries, particularly Russia and China, offer Build-Own-Operate financing where the vendor finances, builds, owns, and operates the plant, with the host country paying per kilowatt-hour over decades. This reduces upfront risk for the host country but creates long-term strategic dependencies.
01
The Energy Deficit
600 million Africans have no access to electricity. Africa's total electricity generation is expected to grow sevenfold by 2050. The continent produces 14 percent of global uranium supply from Namibia, Niger, and South Africa. The resources, the need, and the technology are converging.
02
The SMR Window
SMR technology is reaching commercial readiness at exactly the moment when African energy demand is accelerating. Countries that move now on regulatory preparation, workforce development, and site selection will be positioned to deploy in the early 2030s. Countries that wait face a 10 to 15 year delay from standing start.
03
Industrial Demand as Anchor
Africa's mining sector, growing data centre industry, and manufacturing zones need 24-hour baseload power that solar and wind cannot reliably provide. These industrial consumers are natural anchor buyers for nuclear power. Their PPAs provide the revenue certainty that unlocks financing.
04
The Rwanda Proof-of-Concept
A successful Rwandan SMR deployment would be the first reference plant in a small African grid context. This reduces technology risk perception for every subsequent African program. The commercial and geopolitical value of being first is enormous. Rwanda is not just building a power plant; it is building the template.

LEGAL AND ADVISORY
Nuclear energy projects are among the most legally complex infrastructure transactions in the world. This module maps the full legal and transactional architecture from national law enactment through technology vendor selection, financial close, and construction contracts. Every layer must be in place before a nuclear project can achieve bankability.
COUNSEL
Experienced legal counsel is essential to proper structuring and risk allocation in nuclear transaction documents. The complexity of nuclear law, international liability conventions, EPC structures, and PPA mechanics requires specialised expertise that Rwanda and all newcomer countries must secure early in Phase 2.
National Legal Architecture
FOUNDATIONAL LAW
Comprehensive Nuclear Energy Law
The nuclear law is the legal foundation for the entire programme. It must establish the independent regulatory body, define licensing requirements, set safety and radiation protection standards, govern nuclear waste and decommissioning obligations, establish nuclear liability provisions, and create the legal framework for safeguards implementation. Rwanda's nuclear law was drafted in 2025 and is under legislative review as the priority item identified in the IAEA INIR Phase 1 mission.
PRIORITY ACTIONPHASE 2 GATE
REGULATORY FRAMEWORK
Licensing and Regulatory Policy
Rwanda must develop a complete regulatory framework covering site licensing, construction permits, operating licences, and decommissioning licences. The framework must align with IAEA Safety Standards and the requirements of Milestone 2 of the IAEA Milestones Approach. RAEB serves as the independent regulatory body and must develop the technical competency and legal instruments to exercise its licensing authority. Regulatory inspectors and technical staff capacity is an immediate development priority.
IN PROGRESS
INTERNATIONAL CONVENTIONS
Nuclear Liability Framework
Nuclear liability is governed by international conventions including the Vienna Convention on Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage and the Paris Convention. These instruments establish the principle of strict, exclusive, and limited liability of the operator. Accession to these conventions is typically required by project finance lenders as a condition of financing. Without a clear national liability framework aligned to international conventions, nuclear project finance cannot be structured. Rwanda must complete this legal instrument as part of Phase 2 preparation.
REQUIRED FOR FINANCE
SAFEGUARDS
IAEA Safeguards Agreements
Rwanda must maintain a Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement with the IAEA and is strongly encouraged to sign the Additional Protocol, which provides enhanced inspection rights. The Additional Protocol is a de facto requirement for vendor technology agreements and international financing. No credible nuclear technology vendor will proceed with a legally binding agreement with a country that has not signed the Additional Protocol. This is a non-negotiable element of the international legal architecture for nuclear programmes.
CRITICAL PATH
Transaction Architecture for Nuclear Projects
INTERGOVERNMENTAL AGREEMENT
Host Government Agreement and IGA
Intergovernmental Agreements establish the bilateral framework between the host country and the technology-supplying country. They cover technology transfer terms, financing arrangements, training commitments, waste management obligations, and sovereignty protections. Rwanda has executed an IGA with Russia for the Centre for Nuclear Science and Technology. Future IGAs for SMR deployment will be more complex, covering construction, fuel supply, operations support, and decommissioning across a 60 to 80 year programme lifecycle.
EXAMPLE: RUSSIA IGA 2019
EPC CONTRACT STRUCTURE
Engineering, Procurement and Construction
Nuclear EPC contracts are typically structured on a fixed-price, lump-sum turnkey basis to provide construction cost certainty for project finance lenders. The EPC contractor bears construction risk against a guaranteed completion date and performance obligations. For SMR projects, the EPC scope typically includes nuclear island construction, balance of plant, turbine hall, grid connection, and site preparation. Rwanda's EPC contract will be one of the most significant procurement actions in its history and requires specialist legal and owner engineering support.
PHASE 3 ACTION
POWER PURCHASE AGREEMENT
PPA Structure and Creditworthiness
The Power Purchase Agreement is the revenue contract that underpins project finance. A bankable nuclear PPA must be denominated in a hard currency or include adequate foreign exchange protections, have a term of at least 20 to 25 years matching the debt repayment period, include a creditworthy off-taker (government utility, industrial anchor or a combination), specify capacity payment structures that ensure debt service coverage regardless of dispatch, and include regulatory change protections. Multiple anchor off-takers from Rwanda's mining, data centre, and manufacturing sectors strengthen the credit structure.
BANKABILITY GATE
TECHNOLOGY LICENSING
Vendor Technology and Fuel Supply Agreements
Technology licensing agreements with the selected SMR vendor govern intellectual property rights, proprietary design information access, fuel supply arrangements, spare parts, technical support, and operator training. These agreements must be structured carefully to avoid dependency and to protect Rwanda's long-term operational sovereignty. Fuel supply agreements are particularly important given that uranium enrichment is controlled by a small number of suppliers globally, and supply security must be contractually guaranteed across the operational lifetime of the plant.

POLICY TRACK
First-Loss Equity Structure for African Nuclear
DFI concessional capital at the mezzanine level absorbing first-loss risk provides credit enhancement and improves debt service coverage ratios, making projects more attractive to senior lenders. Rather than a simple software procurement, this proposal positions RAEB as the founding institutional partner of the Creativity in Precision Science initiative, conferring lasting institutional identity on a continental programme.
What RAEB's Investment Delivers
AMS:NOSA Becomes RAEB's Digital Public Engagement Platform
Every RAEB public event, stakeholder engagement, and science communication initiative will have a world-class digital home. The platform carries RAEB's institutional endorsement and extends RAEB's reach to students, investors, journalists, and the public with no additional staff cost.
RAEB Is Named Founding Partner of CPS
In all platform communications, press releases, IAEA submissions, and development partner presentations, Rwanda Atomic Energy Board is the founding institutional partner of Creativity in Precision Science. This is a first-mover advantage that cannot be purchased once another institution takes the position.
A Development-Partner-Friendly Narrative That Raises Additional Funding
CPS is designed to attract co-funding from STEM education foundations, digital innovation grants, climate finance facilities, and creative industry development programmes. RAEB's founding-partner status becomes a credibility anchor that multiplies its initial investment. Every additional dollar raised reduces RAEB's ongoing cost.

The Long-Term Vision
"Rwanda has the opportunity to demonstrate that scientific excellence and human imagination are not opposing forces but complementary engines of innovation. Through AMS:NOSA and the Creativity in Precision Science initiative, RAEB can help establish a new model for engaging citizens, students, innovators, and future scientists through interactive digital experiences that transform complex scientific concepts into accessible and inspiring knowledge."
